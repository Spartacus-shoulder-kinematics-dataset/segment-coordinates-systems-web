Concepts

What the ISB recommendations standardize, and the handful of mathematical ideas needed to read every region page.

1. Global frame and notation

All ISB frames are right-handed and orthonormal. In the neutral (anatomical) posture every segment frame is approximately aligned with the global frame:

X
anterior (forward)
Y
superior (up), i.e. along the long axis of a limb segment, pointing proximally
Z
to the right

Wu & Cavanagh (1995) set this global convention; Wu et al. (2002, 2005) define the segment and joint frames. On this site every 3D view uses these axes directly, in millimetres. Bones and landmarks come from one real subject (BodyParts3D) standing in the anatomical position: each landmark was detected on the bone surface, so the frames show real anatomical misalignments (tibial torsion, scapular protraction, a relaxed hand).

2. Segment coordinate systems (LCS)

Each segment frame is built from palpable or estimated landmarks with the same recipe:

  1. Origin: a landmark or a joint centre (e.g. IJ for the thorax, the hip joint centre for the pelvis and femur).
  2. First axis: the unit vector between two landmarks, e.g. Z = (LM − MM)/‖LM − MM‖ at the ankle, or Y = (GH − mid(EL, EM))/‖…‖ for the humerus.
  3. Second axis: the normal of a plane through three landmarks, obtained by a cross product. The order of the cross product fixes the direction ("pointing anteriorly").
  4. Third axis: the cross product of the first two, which guarantees orthogonality and right-handedness.

The recommendation text usually says "the common line perpendicular to X and Z, pointing cranially". That is the last cross product, and the stated direction only holds if the previous cross products were taken in the right order. The construction steps on each region page make these orders explicit.

Some frames have no landmarks at all: the calcaneus and talus frames coincide with the tibia/fibula frame in the neutral posture and are then carried by the bone. Others borrow an axis from another segment: the clavicle takes the thorax Y axis, and humerus option 2 takes the forearm axis at 90° of elbow flexion.

3. Joint coordinate systems (Grood & Suntay)

Grood & Suntay (1983) describe a joint with three rotation axes that each have a clinical meaning:

  • e1, fixed in the proximal (parent) segment: usually its Z axis, for flexion/extension;
  • e3, fixed in the distal (child) segment: usually its Y axis, for axial (internal/external) rotation;
  • e2, the floating axis, perpendicular to both: e2 = (e3 × e1)/‖e3 × e1‖. It belongs to neither segment and is used for ab/adduction, inversion/eversion and similar rotations.

Because the three axes are defined directly, the three rotations can be described without talking about an order. ISB adopted this construction for the ankle, hip and spine (2002) and for the elbow, wrist and hand (2005).

4. A JCS is an intrinsic Euler/Cardan sequence

With Rp and Rc the parent and child frames (columns = axes in global coordinates), the orientation of the child in the parent is R = RpT Rc. For the lower-limb JCS,

R = RZ(α) · RX(β) · RY(γ)

is an intrinsic ZXY Cardan sequence: α about the parent Z (= e1), β about the once-rotated X′ (= e2, the floating axis), γ about the child Y (= e3). Decomposing R with this sequence gives exactly the Grood & Suntay angles, which is how every joint on this site is computed. The shoulder uses YXZ for the girdle (SC, AC, ST) and YXY for the humerus (plane of elevation, elevation, axial rotation).

Floating axis by cross product: for cyclic Cardan sequences (XYZ, YZX, ZXY) e2 = e3 × e1, for the others e2 = e1 × e3. For Euler sequences the cross product changes sign with the middle angle, so the site computes e2 as "the second rotation axis after the first rotation", which is always well defined.

5. Gimbal lock

When the middle angle reaches the singular value, e1 and e3 become parallel and only the sum (or difference) of the first and third angles is defined. Cardan sequences lock at β = ±90°, which lower-limb joints rarely reach. Euler sequences lock at β = 0° or 180°. For the ISB humerus (YXY) the lock is at zero elevation, the arm hanging along the body, so plane of elevation and axial rotation are unstable in that common posture (Šenk & Chèze 2006). Alternatives such as XZY have been proposed for the glenohumeral joint (Phadke et al. 2011).

Every joint card shows a gimbal margin, |cos β| for Cardan and |sin β| for Euler sequences, with a warning below sin 10°.

6. Signs and clinical names

The raw angles follow the right-hand rule about the axis as defined: with Z to the right, a positive rotation about Z tilts X up towards Y. The clinical name of the positive direction depends on where the distal segment is:

  • hip, elbow, wrist, fingers: a positive Z rotation is flexion (the distal segment swings forward);
  • knee: a positive Z rotation is extension, so knee flexion is reported as −α (Grood & Suntay give flexion as positive);
  • ankle: positive is dorsiflexion; spine: positive is extension;
  • humerus (YXY): elevation is negative, and a plane of elevation of 0° is abduction, 90° forward flexion.

Each joint card lists the positive/negative names, and they are checked by the site's tests.

7. The left side

The recommendations are written for the right side. The left side is the mirror image through the sagittal plane, M = diag(1, 1, −1). The mirror image of a right-handed frame is left-handed, so frames cannot simply be mirrored. Two equivalent practices are used:

  1. Keep the ISB orientation on both sides (X anterior, Y superior, Z to the right): Rleft = M Rright M. Joint angles then satisfy Rrel,left = M Rrel,right M, which keeps rotations about Z and flips rotations about X and Y. Clinical left angles are the raw angles multiplied by +1 for Z and −1 for X or Y (for ZXY: +, −, −; for YXY: −, −, −).
  2. Mirror the left data (z → −z) and process it as a right side.

Both give the same clinical angles. Two details follow. First, applying the right-side formulas literally to left landmarks flips some axes, because every cross product of mirrored vectors changes sign; the Left view of each segment shows which axes need flipping. Second, for Euler sequences the preferred sign of the middle angle flips too, because it is a rotation about X. The site uses practice 1; the SPARTACUS project applies the same M R M rule to align left-side literature data.

8. Translations

Grood & Suntay also define three clinical translations: the position of the distal origin relative to the proximal origin, projected on e1 (medial–lateral shift), e2 (anterior–posterior drawer) and e3 (compression–distraction). Because e1 and e3 are not perpendicular in general, the (e1, e2, e3) basis is not orthogonal and these are not Cartesian components. The playgrounds on this site rotate the distal segment about a fixed pivot and do not model translations.

9. Practical pitfalls

  • Neutral posture is not zero. In the anatomical position the shoulder girdle angles are far from zero (clavicle retracted ~20°, scapula protracted ~30°). Use Anatomical posture on the joint cards to see them.
  • Short landmark bases amplify errors. The humeral epicondyles are ~8 cm apart, so a 5 mm error rotates humerus option 1 by several degrees about Y; option 2 uses the forearm as a longer lever.
  • Axis cross-talk. A misplaced flexion axis (e1) leaks flexion into the floating-axis angle: the classic "knee varus/valgus artefact" in gait.
  • Joint centres are estimated. The hip joint centre and GH are not palpable; regression or functional methods change the femur and humerus frames.
  • Sequence dependence. The same orientation gives very different numbers in another sequence (see Sequences), so always report the sequence.
